Charter School Insurance: What to Carry, Who Requires It, and What Drives the Cost

A plain-English guide for charter school business managers, CFOs and boards. It covers the coverages a charter school needs, where the requirements come from, and what actually moves the premium. Written by brokers who have worked only with charter schools since 2011.

The short answer. Most charter schools carry a core package of general liability, educators legal liability (which includes D&O and employment practices liability), sexual abuse & molestation coverage, property, workers’ compensation, an umbrella, auto (including hired and non-owned auto), crime, cyber, and student accident, plus an employee benefits program. The exact limits are usually set by three documents: your charter contract, your facility lease, and any bond or loan agreement.

Why charter school insurance is different

A traditional district usually buys insurance as part of a large pool, with a risk manager on staff and, in many states, governmental immunities that cap what it can be sued for. A charter school is typically its own nonprofit corporation with its own board. Depending on your state, it may not share the district’s coverage or all of the district’s legal protections. It is also usually small enough that one bad claim can affect the operating budget.

On top of that, a charter school answers to more outside parties than most organizations its size. The authorizer can revoke the charter. The landlord or lender owns or finances the building. Bondholders hold covenants that name specific coverages. Each of them writes insurance requirements into a contract. A policy built for a generic nonprofit or a private school often misses at least one of them.

That’s why charter school insurance is best treated as its own category: standard commercial coverages, configured to meet a specific set of contracts and exposures.

What a charter school needs to carry

Below are the coverages that make up a typical charter school program. Not every school needs every line at the same limit, but every school should make a deliberate decision about each one.

General liability

General liability covers bodily injury and property damage claims from third parties: a visitor slips in the hallway, a parent is hurt at a school event, a student’s injury leads to a lawsuit. It is the coverage nearly every authorizer and landlord requires by name. Check whether your policy includes or excludes athletics, field trips, before- and after-school programs, and use of your facility by outside groups.

Educators legal liability (D&O and employment practices)

Educators legal liability (ELL), sometimes called school leaders errors & omissions, is the management liability policy for schools. It typically combines directors & officers (D&O) coverage for decisions made by the board and leadership, employment practices liability (EPL) for claims like wrongful termination, discrimination, harassment and retaliation, and educators errors & omissions for claims tied to educational decisions, such as special education services, discipline, or enrollment.

For many charter schools this is the coverage most likely to be used. Two details matter a great deal: the retroactive date, and whether your umbrella actually sits over this policy. See our guide to management liability for charter school boards.

Sexual abuse & molestation

Any organization that serves minors needs this coverage, and authorizers increasingly ask for it explicitly. It is often written with a sublimit inside the general liability policy, and that sublimit can be far lower than the main limit. Know the number, know whether defense costs erode it, and know whether the umbrella extends over it.

Property

Property insurance covers the building (if you own it or your lease makes you responsible for it), your furniture, technology and equipment, and the income you lose when a covered loss closes a campus. Key items to check are replacement cost versus actual cash value, water damage and flood sublimits, and extra expense coverage that pays for temporary space while a damaged building is repaired.

Workers’ compensation

Workers’ compensation pays medical costs and lost wages for employees injured on the job. In most states it is required by law once you have employees, and the rules differ by state (Texas is a well-known exception, where coverage is elective). Premium is driven by payroll and job classification, so an accurate payroll estimate and correct class codes matter at both renewal and audit.

Umbrella / excess liability

An umbrella adds limits on top of your general liability, auto, employer’s liability, and, ideally, educators legal liability and abuse coverage. Authorizers and lenders commonly require one. The most expensive gap we see is an umbrella that doesn’t extend over educators legal liability, which leaves the school’s most-used liability policy without the extra limits the board thinks it has.

Auto and hired & non-owned auto

If the school owns buses or vans, it needs commercial auto. Even if it owns nothing, staff and volunteers drive personal cars on school business every week: field trips, bank deposits, supply runs. Hired & non-owned auto protects the school when one of those trips leads to a claim. It is inexpensive and often missing.

Cyber and crime

Schools hold student records, staff payroll data, and family financial information, and they move money by ACH. Cyber insurance responds to data breaches, ransomware, and the notification and forensic costs that follow. Crime insurance responds to employee theft and, if endorsed, to social engineering fraud such as a fake vendor invoice. The two overlap less than most people assume. See crime vs. cyber coverage for charter schools.

Student accident

Student accident insurance pays medical bills for students injured during school activities, usually on an excess basis over the family’s own health insurance. It covers the gap when a family has high deductibles or no coverage at all, and it often prevents a medical bill from becoming a lawsuit. Many schools carry a base plan for all students and an enhanced plan for athletics.

Other coverages to consider

  • Active assailant / violent event coverage for crisis response, counseling, and business interruption. See active assailant coverage.
  • Fiduciary liability if the school sponsors a retirement plan.
  • Equipment breakdown for HVAC, boilers, and electrical systems.
  • Pollution / environmental for older buildings and campuses near industrial sites.

Employee benefits and health insurance for charter schools

For most charter schools, benefits are the second-largest budget line after salaries, and the place where the most money is lost without anyone noticing. Charter schools often have a younger, smaller workforce than districts and no access to the state or district health pool. The result is fully insured small-group plans with renewals that are hard to predict and claims data the school never sees.

A charter school benefits program usually includes medical, dental, vision, life and disability, and an employee assistance program. It is designed around three questions: how the plan is funded, how much control the school has over its data and pharmacy spending, and how much administrative work it puts on a small HR team.

Who sets the requirements: authorizers, landlords, and lenders

Most charter schools don’t choose their minimum insurance limits from scratch. They inherit them from contracts. The three main sources each care about something different:

WhoWhere it’s writtenWhat they typically ask for
Authorizer Charter contract, state statute, or the authorizer’s insurance policy Minimum limits for general liability, educators legal liability, abuse & molestation, auto, and umbrella; workers’ comp as required by law; the authorizer named as additional insured; certificates delivered by a deadline
Landlord Facility lease General liability with the landlord as additional insured; property coverage on whatever the lease makes the school responsible for; waiver of subrogation; notice of cancellation
Lender / bondholders Loan agreement, bond indenture, or continuing covenants Replacement-cost property coverage on the financed building, business interruption or rental value coverage, the lender named as mortgagee or loss payee, minimum carrier financial ratings, and an annual insurance compliance certification

Two practical points follow. First, these documents often conflict or overlap, and the strictest one governs. A program that satisfies the authorizer can still breach a bond covenant. Second, the requirements change when the school grows: a new campus, a new lease, or a bond refinancing should trigger an insurance review, not just a certificate request. If a lender is asking you to certify compliance, see our bond compliance review.

The school’s board sets the last requirement: what level of risk it is willing to keep. Minimum contract limits are a floor, not a recommendation.

What drives the cost of charter school insurance

Business managers usually want a number. The honest answer is that two schools with the same enrollment can pay very different premiums. These factors explain most of the difference:

  1. Enrollment and payroll. Liability pricing scales with students; workers’ comp scales with payroll and job classifications.
  2. Buildings. Replacement value, construction type, age, sprinklers and alarms, and location. Campuses in hail, wind, wildfire or flood zones cost more to insure, sometimes far more.
  3. Claims history. Underwriters look at roughly five years of losses, especially employment and abuse claims.
  4. Programs and operations. Athletics (especially contact sports), transportation, early childhood, residential programs, and community use of the facility all add exposure.
  5. Limits and deductibles. Higher limits cost more; higher deductibles cost less. The right balance depends on the school’s reserves.
  6. Risk controls. Background checks, abuse-prevention training, written HR policies, and cyber controls such as multi-factor authentication can affect both price and eligibility.
  7. How the program is placed. A school placed with a carrier or program that understands charter schools is underwritten differently from one shopped as a generic nonprofit.
  8. The market cycle. Property and liability pricing moves with the broader insurance market, regardless of how a single school performs.

The fastest way to know whether your premium is reasonable is to compare it with similar schools. That’s what our renewal report card and free benchmark review are for.

How to choose a charter school insurance broker

Ask any broker these questions before you hand over your renewal:

  • How many charter schools do you work with, and in which states?
  • Have you read our charter contract, lease, and bond documents, and will you map our coverage to them?
  • Does our umbrella extend over educators legal liability and abuse & molestation?
  • What are our sublimits for abuse, water damage, and social engineering fraud?
  • Are you independent? How many carriers will you approach for us?
  • What do you do between renewals?

Charter school insurance FAQs

Do charter schools need D&O insurance?

Yes. A charter school’s board members can be sued personally over governance decisions, and most authorizers and lenders expect directors & officers coverage. For schools, D&O is usually part of an educators legal liability policy that also covers employment practices and educational decisions. Buying D&O alone typically leaves the more common employment and student-related claims uncovered.

What insurance does our authorizer require?

It is written in your charter contract, your state’s charter statute, or a separate insurance policy issued by the authorizer. Most specify minimum limits for general liability, educators legal liability, abuse & molestation, auto and umbrella, require workers’ compensation as the law requires, and ask to be named as an additional insured. Requirements vary widely, so check the document itself.

How much does charter school insurance cost?

It depends mainly on enrollment, payroll, buildings and location, claims history, and the limits you carry. Two schools of the same size can differ significantly. Rather than relying on a rule of thumb, compare your current premium and coverage against similar schools. We do that as a free benchmark review.

Are charter schools covered by the school district’s insurance?

Usually not. Most charter schools are separate legal entities and must buy their own coverage, even when the district is the authorizer. Some states and some district-authorized schools have pooled or shared arrangements, so confirm in writing rather than assuming.

Do we need workers’ compensation if we only have a few employees?

In most states, yes. Workers’ comp is required by law once you have employees, with state-specific thresholds and exceptions. Your authorizer may also require it regardless of state law. Remember that stipend coaches and part-time staff may count as employees.

What is the difference between general liability and educators legal liability?

General liability covers bodily injury and property damage, like a fall on the playground. Educators legal liability covers financial harm from decisions and actions: employment disputes, board decisions, special education and discipline claims. Most lawsuits against charter schools fall under the second, which is why both are needed.

Does our umbrella cover educators legal liability?

Not always, and it is one of the most common gaps we find. Many umbrellas sit only over general liability, auto, and employer’s liability. Check the schedule of underlying insurance on your umbrella policy; if educators legal liability isn’t listed, those extra limits don’t apply to it.

When should we start our insurance renewal?

Start 90 to 120 days before expiration. That leaves time to update enrollment, payroll and building values, check requirements against any new lease or financing, and give the broker room to approach more than one carrier. Starting 30 days out usually means accepting whatever the incumbent offers.
